Session 1: Symbols by Hand

Goal: Learn what a mission patch is. Draw a set of symbols that answers: What message would Baruch send into space?

01Overview

02What happens in microgravity?

10 minutes · The International Space Station, student experiments, microgravity research, the goals of SSEP.

Microgravity: the condition in which people and objects appear to be weightless and float. It happens in orbit, where everything falls together (NASA).

What changes when gravity is removed?

The ISS is in orbit: gravity isn’t gone. At the station it is still about 90% as strong as on the ground. So things there are falling. But the station falls too, at the same speed, so nothing falls toward its floor. That is why things float inside. The station also moves sideways so fast, about 17,500 mph, that it keeps falling around Earth instead of into it (NASA).

What changes is what gravity usually does: hot air stops rising, heavy things stop sinking, bubbles stop floating up. So shapes change. A burning droplet makes a round flame instead of a teardrop (NASA). Crystals grow bigger and more even (IML).

What questions can only be investigated in space?

Questions about how things behave when gravity stops shaping them. Flames, crystals, seeds and cells all act differently in space, and on the station that lasts for months. Student experiments in SSEP study topics like these (ISS National Lab). So scientists can ask: How does a flame burn when hot air can’t rise? How does a crystal grow when nothing sinks? How does a seed sprout with no up or down?

Why should students conduct research beyond Earth?

In SSEP, students from fifth grade through college design real experiments that fly to the space station. They go through the same steps as scientists: proposal, review, flight (ISS National Lab). Baruch’s experiment flies in Spring 2027. It takes a community to launch an experiment into space.

03What is a mission patch?

10 minutes · Apollo, Shuttle and ISS expedition patches. Mission patches are not advertisements. They are symbolic artifacts that tell the story of a mission and the people who make it possible.

How do these patches communicate mission identity?

The crew designs its own patch. Gordon Cooper started this with Gemini 5 in 1965 (AIGA Eye on Design). One symbol stands for the goal: Apollo 11’s eagle carries an olive branch, landing on the Moon in peace. Some patches show the spacecraft; others stand for the science.

What symbols repeat?

Earth, the Moon, stars, orbits, spacecraft, national symbols like the eagle, and the mission name running around the edge.

Why are circles common?

A circle reads as a planet, an orbit, a window or a seal. It has no top or bottom, and it stays clear when small.

Why are crews often represented symbolically?

Apollo 11 left the crew’s names off. The mission was “for all humankind” and stood for the thousands of people who worked on it (AIGA, Sky at Night).

05Activities

75 minutes

  • 15 min: Visual translation game: One minute each to draw curiosity, discovery, weightlessness, collaboration, experimentation.
  • 20 min: Symbol mapping: Start from the question. Make two maps:
    1. Space research: orbit, ISS, discovery, microgravity, experiment.
    2. Baruch community: New York City, diversity, learning, opportunity, student research.
    Find where the two maps connect.
  • 20 min: Rapid concept generation: Six thumbnails: geometric, narrative, abstract, symbolic, minimal, unexpected. The goal is exploration rather than refinement.
  • 25 min: The competition rules and the two-week plan.

06Homework

3–4 hours

  • 2 hours: Finish your symbols. Draw each one on its own. Write what each one means.
  • 30 min: Photograph each symbol and thumbnail on white paper, in even light.
